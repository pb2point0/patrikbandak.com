How I got here...

Relentless Curiosity

I have been building computers and teaching myself software since I was nine years old. If you’ve gotten to know me, you might have heard me say that. It is an easy way to explain something that has followed me through nearly everything I have done: when I encounter something I do not understand, I tend to take it apart until I do.

In fifth grade, our computer class was assigned a website project built entirely in HTML. I went home, coded mine that night, saved it to a floppy disk, and brought it back to school the next day. Once I had finished the assignment, I started helping my friends with theirs so we could all get on to something more fun.

Around the same time, I took apart my first computer, a Dell, and managed to put it back together with a few screws left over. It still worked. Before long, I had rebuilt it inside a Thermaltake case, replaced components, cut into the case with a Dremel, modified the cooling system, and posted the finished computer on Twisted Mods. I was young enough that most of the people I was talking to had no idea they were talking to a kid. That was probably part of the appeal. I liked being around people who knew much more than I did and learning enough to participate in the conversation.

Photoshop came next. I made logos, forum signatures, and whatever else gave me an excuse to figure out another tool. None of this felt like preparation for anything. I was just relentlessly curious, and once I understood something, I usually wanted to see what I could do with it.

That curiosity was not limited to computers. My dad coached, and I grew up in a family that was intensely competitive but equally supportive. I trained alongside athletes several years older than me, played soccer, and eventually got heavily into competitive paintball. I also started working young, first helping in my parents’ warehouse and later refereeing at a paintball field. A few years after that, I helped build Raza Field, San Diego Dynasty’s own field, which at one point had me thirty feet in the air on a boom lift installing safety netting.

I did not think much about the pattern at the time. I liked being useful, I liked learning from people who were better than I was, and I was rarely bothered by being the least experienced person in the room.

Starting Over

I lost my best friend during my freshman year of high school and failed the year. The following year, after moving to a charter school, I earned a 4.0. I never completed a traditional junior year because my family’s life was increasingly divided between the United States, Germany, and Sweden, and I moved to Munich.

My first stretch there lasted about six months. I studied German at the Goethe-Institut and attended a German high school before returning to California, where I earned my GED. About a year later, I went back to Germany for what was supposed to be a visit. I was offered a temporary position at a Montessori school for a couple of weeks, it went well, and they asked me to stay for the rest of the school year. I did, spending another six months in Munich working with the students and helping teach English before returning to the United States at eighteen.

There was a fair amount of reinvention packed into those years. Schools changed, countries changed, languages changed, and the people around me changed. Starting over stopped feeling particularly dramatic because I had done it enough times to know that the uncomfortable part eventually passed. I learned to pay attention, figure out the environment I had walked into, and find a way to contribute before I completely understood it.

That has probably been more useful in adulthood than I appreciated at the time.

Learning How to Think

Back in California, I sold shoes at Robert Wayne, worked as a valet in Coronado, and eventually found my way into banking as a teller. I was still figuring out what I wanted to do, and for a while work and school were simply happening alongside each other.

One of the classes that changed that was English 110 with Professor Jendian at Grossmont College. He was not easy to impress. He was adamant that every word in an essay, every word in an advertisement, and even every word on one of those hotel placards asking you to reuse your towels was there for a reason. He drilled rhetoric into us, including a line I still hear in my head from time to time: “If you don’t study rhetoric, you will fall victim to it.”

Around then, I was taking the trolley to work and would occasionally kill time at a Barnes & Noble nearby. One day I wandered into the philosophy section and picked up Aristotle’s Poetics and Rhetoric. That led to the Nicomachean Ethics, Marcus Aurelius’ Meditations, Plato’s Apology, and a much broader interest in philosophy.

I was less interested in finding the philosophy I agreed with than in understanding the arguments. Why did someone believe what they believed? What assumptions were underneath the conclusion? What changed when the same question was viewed from somewhere else?

Years later, when I encountered David Foster Wallace’s This Is Water, his description of education as learning how to think felt familiar. The value was not simply knowing more things. It was becoming more conscious of what I noticed, how quickly I formed conclusions, and how much of the world I was interpreting through assumptions I had never really examined.

When I transferred to Saint Mary’s College of California, Professor Kolhede recommended during my first semester that I become the Business Administration tutor. I ended up being the only one for the two years I was there, sometimes tutoring material from classes I was taking myself. It reinforced something I had discovered years earlier while helping friends with HTML: explaining something to someone else is one of the fastest ways to discover whether you actually understand it.

By then, living between cultures had become a fairly normal part of my life as well. I am a Swedish citizen, had spent significant time in Munich, and later worked in Abu Dhabi. Those experiences made it difficult to assume that my own way of seeing something was the obvious one. Context matters, and intelligent people can look at exactly the same situation and come away with very different interpretations.

I ultimately graduated magna cum laude, but what stayed with me more than the degree was the habit of asking better questions.

Systems

I started on the teller line and moved through personal banking, small-business banking, commercial banking, customer success, treasury operations, complex implementations, and eventually much deeper into product and technology. There was never a master plan behind that progression. Each role exposed another part of the business that I wanted to understand.

For several years, part of my work involved helping bankers develop their business acumen and become more comfortable working with clients. I enjoyed it for many of the same reasons I had enjoyed tutoring. Teaching forced me to make things clear, while working with people who approached the job differently exposed gaps in my own thinking.

Implementations changed my perspective again because they forced me to see more of the bank at once. A client might describe what they wanted in business terms, while Product saw a capability, Operations saw the process required to support it, and Technology saw the architecture and dependencies underneath it. Information Security, Risk, Compliance, Legal, or another group could then introduce a constraint that was every bit as real as the original client need.

I found myself increasingly drawn to that overlap.

Much of my work today lives somewhere between banking, product, technology, and operations. I might begin with a client problem, a broken process, or an idea for a new capability, but solving it usually means understanding enough of several different systems to see how they affect one another. The work has gradually expanded from implementing what already exists to questioning the way the underlying product, workflow, or operating model works in the first place.

That is also how I got deeper into automation. Repeated manual work has a way of making the design problems underneath it very visible. I started building tools and workflows to remove some of that work, which led naturally into broader product conversations and, more recently, AI.

AI interests me for many of the same reasons computers interested me when I was a kid. I want to understand what it can do, where the limits are, and what changes when you put it into the hands of people trying to get real work done. The difference now is that I spend much more time thinking about the surrounding system.

Inside a bank, a prototype working is only the beginning. You need to know where the information came from, whether it is current, who should have access to it, where human judgment belongs, what happens when the output is wrong, and who owns the process when the novelty wears off. I have become more interested in governance as the technology has become more capable, because the interesting challenge is no longer simply whether something can be built. It is whether it can become a reliable part of how an organization operates.

I still like getting into the details myself. I build things, work through APIs, databases, interfaces, and workflows, and use AI to get into unfamiliar technical territory faster than I could before. I also know that I do not need to be the deepest technical expert in every room. What matters more is understanding enough to work well with the people who are, while keeping sight of the customer, the business problem, and all the other parts of the system that determine whether an idea survives contact with reality.

The systems are considerably larger than the Dell I took apart when I was nine, but the instinct is pretty much the same.

Connecting the Dots

Steve Jobs once said, “You can’t connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards.” I have always liked that line because most of the things that shaped me did not look especially connected while they were happening.

Learning HTML, rebuilding computers, spending hours in Photoshop, moving between countries, reading philosophy, tutoring, working in a bank branch, learning commercial banking, getting pulled into complex implementations, building internal tools, and eventually working across product, technology, operations, and AI all felt like separate interests or separate stages of my life.

They make more sense together now.

I like understanding how things work, especially when understanding them requires looking at the problem from more than one direction. I enjoy working with people who know things I do not, and I am comfortable going deep enough into something unfamiliar to participate meaningfully in the conversation. Once I understand the system, I tend to start noticing the places where it could work better.

That is probably why I have never felt much need to fit neatly into one professional discipline. Banking, product, technology, operations, design, communication, and organizational behavior all interest me, and the work I enjoy most usually happens somewhere between them. I am interested in the whole kit and kaboodle, not because I need to own every piece of it, but because the pieces rarely behave independently.

Long term, I want to build and run businesses. I do not know exactly what form that takes yet, and I do not think I need to. Whether I eventually build something of my own or help run something larger, I care more about the work, the responsibility, and the opportunity to make something better than I do about the title attached to it.

Looking backwards, the dots already connect enough for me to trust the pattern.
