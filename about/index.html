How I got here…

Relentless Curiosity

I have been building computers and teaching myself software since I was nine years old. That is an easy way to explain something that has followed me through nearly everything I have done: when I encounter something I do not understand, I tend to take it apart until I do.

In fifth grade, our computer class was assigned a website project built entirely in HTML. I coded mine at home in one night, saved it to a floppy disk, and brought it to school the next day. Once I finished, I helped my friends build theirs. Computers were social for me too. We hauled our PCs to LAN parties, and by twelve I was playing Counter-Strike competitively in CAL-Main. Around then I also rebuilt my Dell into a Thermaltake case and became active in one of the better-known computer-modding communities online, where I traded ideas with some of the most skilled people in the scene. Most of them had no idea they were talking to a kid.

That curiosity occasionally got me into trouble. In high school, computer class moved much more slowly than I did. I would finish the assignments, get bored, and start exploring what else I could do on the school network. Eventually I figured out how to send a message to every computer on campus, so naturally I sent everyone an urgent message telling them to shut down their computers immediately. I was banned from using school computers for the rest of the year. I learned my lesson. These days I’m much more white hat than deviant.

Sports were developing another side of me. My dad coached, and I grew up in a family that was intensely competitive but equally supportive. Soccer, speed training, and plyometrics were simply part of life. I regularly trained alongside athletes several years older than me and sometimes outperformed high school players in the speed drills. I found out early that being the least experienced person in the room was not necessarily a disadvantage. It was often where I learned the fastest.

I started working early too, in my parents’ warehouse in San Diego. I helped unload shipping containers, and since I was small enough to fit where the adults could not, I would climb all the way into the back and push boxes out. Between loads, I could often be found on the third story of the scaffolding, lying on top of the stacks and playing my Game Boy.

Then came paintball. I went with my brother and his friends and worked as a referee at Mr. Paintball in Escondido. At twelve, one of my more memorable assignments was refereeing more than twenty officers from the Escondido Police Department for the day. They had a blast, and so did I. A few years later I was playing more competitively and helped build Raza Field, San Diego Dynasty’s own field, which included installing safety netting from a boom lift roughly thirty feet in the air.

At the time, none of this felt especially unusual. I was learning that competition, encouragement, and high expectations could coexist.

Starting Over

My freshman year of high school, I joined the water polo team and met the person who became my best friend. He was a junior, and sometimes he would pick me up and take me to school. It felt like I was being seen and looked out for. He had a Jesus figurine on his stick shift and would announce, “I have the power of Jesus in my hands!”

He died later that school year. I failed freshman year.

The following year I moved to a charter school and earned a 4.0. I never completed a traditional junior year, though. My family’s life was increasingly divided between the United States, Germany, and Sweden, and I moved to Munich. My first stretch there lasted about six months. I studied German at the Goethe-Institut and attended a German high school before returning to California, where I earned my GED.

About a year later I went back to Germany for what was supposed to be a visit. A Montessori school offered me a temporary position for a couple of weeks, and it went well enough that they asked me to stay for the rest of the school year. I did. I spent another six months in Munich working with the students and helping teach English, then returned to the United States at eighteen. The kids probably taught me more German than I taught them English. It is hard not to learn when you have twenty-five children talking to you and asking, “Kannst du mir mit meinen Schuhen helfen?” or “Bist du der Schiedsrichter oder der Torwart?” Natürlich war ich immer der Torwart.

Those years gave me a lot of practice at starting over. New schools, new countries, new languages, and very different groups of people became normal fairly early, and I got comfortable walking into situations where I did not know everything yet. It would not be the last time I started over.

Learning How to Think

Back in California I sold shoes at Robert Wayne, worked as a valet in Coronado, and eventually found my way into banking as a teller. I showed up to that job in TJ Maxx specials that were far too large for me. I moved from part-time to full-time after three months and into a hybrid teller and personal-banking role nine months later. Along the way I noticed how much of a customer’s experience came down to small, repeatable behaviors. Valeting at a resort in Coronado had shown me five-star service up close, so I started applying the same principles in the branch, and within a relatively short period I helped move our professional-treatment score from 70% to 96%.

None of those jobs seemed related at the time, but together they gave me an education in people. I also found that I liked being responsible for making something work well for someone else.

College took those instincts in several directions at once. A rhetoric course introduced me to Aristotle, and I became fascinated by the way he connected persuasion, character, logic, and judgment. I read my way from the Rhetoric and the Poetics into the Nicomachean Ethics, then on to Marcus Aurelius and Plato, and came out with a much broader interest in how people reason, communicate, and make decisions.

I also met professors who changed the way I thought about learning itself. Professor Kolhede used to call me his “philosopher king,” but more importantly he pushed me. He encouraged me to become a tutor, and for the two years I attended Saint Mary’s I was the only tutor on campus dedicated to business administration. Teaching made me understand ideas differently. It was not enough to know the answer myself. I had to know something well enough to see where another person was getting lost and explain it in a way that made sense to them. If I could help someone else use an idea, I knew I understood it.

I struggled with one of my economics professors. He was brilliant, but I had a hard time with the way he taught, so for a while I sat through two sections of his macroeconomics class back to back. I took notes during the first and just listened during the second. Another economics professor, the kind who threw the textbook in the trash on the first day, pulled me in, and I started dropping into his class whenever I could. Eventually I asked him if I could switch.

“You’re an idiot,” he told me. “He is the smartest person on this campus, and if you can’t learn from him, then you need to change your learning style.”

So I did. I figured out how to learn from him, and I went on to graduate magna cum laude.

Some of the most important parts of college happened well outside the classroom. I worked as the graphic designer for the Intercultural Center, creating materials for student programs across campus. The design work was fun, but what mattered more was being embedded in a community that brought together students with remarkably different cultures, backgrounds, identities, and experiences. I got to know people I might never otherwise have met.

During the first year of the Middle Eastern Club, I served as controller and helped put together Cultural Night, an event called Arabian Nights. Each cultural club hosted its own night, and these were substantial events that often drew more than one hundred people. I worked on the planning, designed the event materials, helped run the audio and lighting, and, somehow, ended up dancing in it too. Arabian Nights won Student Program of the Year. I liked that kind of work because there was no clean boundary around my role, and I did not think much about whether those things were supposed to belong together.

Near the end of college, an internship took me to Abu Dhabi, and the work there sat much closer to private equity than to banking. I helped with two launches: Jalboot, a private ferry and water-taxi service, and a commercial laundry facility. I did due diligence on a medical practice we were considering acquiring, and we ultimately passed on it. I am a Swedish citizen with family in Sweden and Germany, so moving between cultures already felt normal to me. Between Munich and Abu Dhabi, I learned to listen before assuming I understand the environment I have entered, and to expect intelligent people to approach the same problem very differently. The more different the room, the more interesting I tend to find it.

Systems

Pace

After college I joined Bank of America as a personal banker at 50 California Street in San Francisco’s Financial District, and I loved that environment. I finished at more than 200% of target in some categories, and within nine months I was promoted to Small Business Banker, skipping the consultant role in between. My territory covered five branches in the district, with a home base at Market and New Montgomery, the West Coast flagship.

The role also changed how I thought about performance. I coached three Small Business Consultants and roughly fifteen personal bankers. I wrote calling scripts, ran role-play sessions, helped people prepare for conversations, and made myself available when someone needed help getting a client across the finish line. I was still responsible for my own number, and I wanted to crush it, but I began to understand that the more interesting problem was not how to perform well myself. It was how to create the conditions for other people to perform well: clear expectations, useful practice, timely feedback, real support, and enough trust to let someone improve.

In 2018 I moved to Chase as a Business Relationship Manager, at a time when San Francisco felt particularly electric. The Warriors had won multiple championships and Chase Center was taking shape in Mission Bay. My portfolio was about a hundred businesses with $3 million to $20 million in revenue. I was their primary advisor on deposits, lending, and treasury, which meant plenty of conversations with owners about cash flow and working capital. Just as often I was working with treasury specialists, credit, and operations to structure solutions that fit each business, then making sure onboarding and implementation went smoothly.

In December 2019 I transferred with Chase to San Diego and took a long-planned three-week trip through the Philippines. I got back just before the pandemic changed everything. When PPP arrived, I threw myself into it. I learned the underwriting requirements, governance, and processes as fast as I could and then pushed to move requests through accurately. For stretches of time I was working sixteen-hour days, because businesses were waiting for money they needed and I felt a responsibility to move quickly without cutting corners. Eventually the pace caught up with me, and I burned out.

Balance

About a year later I resigned from Chase and took time off. I eventually joined a SaaS company and was later laid off in a round of restructuring. Unexpectedly, I felt relieved. I had been exhausted for a while, and the interruption gave me permission to ask a question I had not really asked since college: what would I learn if my next decision did not have to look like a continuation of the last one?

My answer was science. I have always been curious about anatomy, physiology, movement, and what the human body is capable of, so physical therapy seemed like a natural direction. It would have meant roughly five years of school, but I figured I could start with the general science requirements and see where they led.

Meanwhile my aunt was moving back to Delaware. She knew I was figuring out my next move and asked whether I wanted to come with her and help her out. Why not? I sold almost everything, shipped a box, and brought two suitcases.

A few months later I met my wife. When she heard about the five-year physical-therapy plan, she suggested a more immediate experiment: get a massage-therapy license. I spent the next ten months doing exactly that, and it was a perfect fit. I studied everything the program required and got a great deal of hands-on experience along the way. I threw myself into the material, but this time the system was the human body.

Building

When it became clear I needed another pivot, I returned to banking strategically. At Chase I had always enjoyed working with the treasury specialists because they worked where clients, products, operations, and technology met. When Customers Bank opened a position in treasury operations, I went for it, and in many ways I started my career over again. I had years of banking experience, but I was stepping into a part of the business I did not yet understand deeply, and I was in a hurry to close that gap. I wanted exposure. I wanted difficult work and the responsibility that came with it. I wanted to understand how everything connected, and I wanted to become genuinely good at it.

I asked questions constantly. I volunteered for work I did not entirely know how to do yet, and I found people who understood parts of the business better than I did and asked them to teach me. I also sought mentorship from senior leaders I respected. The best of those conversations were not comfortable in the flattering sense. They exposed weaknesses I had not fully seen, helped me recognize strengths I sometimes took for granted, and gave me larger problems to think about than the ones immediately in front of me. As the problems got larger, so did my curiosity about the systems that produced them.

Then AI changed what I could build. A colleague introduced me to Codex. He will say he barely showed me anything, but he sparked the fire, and much of the building that followed started there. At first, I prototyped mostly by feel, leaning heavily on AI to build what I needed, and for those early tools that was fine. The onboarding workflows were a different kind of work. Unlike those prototypes, they had to run inside the bank’s own environment and hold up in real use. I learned how they operated by asking questions, and the answers pointed me toward the Microsoft stack. AI became more of a tutor than a builder. I used it to plan the architecture and learn how each one worked, and eventually I understood them well enough to build and modify them directly. Over time I built individual tools, then platforms that brought those tools together. I automated processes that had taken hours down to seconds, and I began turning one-off work into repeatable workflows and repeatable workflows into products.

Much of it came from impatience. Waiting weeks for a relatively simple Salesforce process to work its way through an Azure DevOps queue drove me crazy, so instead of holding out for the ideal solution I asked what was possible, safely, with the tools and access I already had. In one case I designed the operational flow myself. Power Automate moved the work into trackers I built in SharePoint Lists, with Power BI dashboards updating automatically on top of them. I built the first version over a weekend, and before that weekend I had essentially no experience with any of those tools. After enough projects like that, the question stopped being whether I already knew a particular piece of software. Give me the problem, the constraints, and the systems available, and I can learn the rest.

But the faster I got at prototyping, the more aware I became of what I did not know. Inside a bank I could work with enterprise systems without ever having unrestricted access to the layers that run them. I did not want lack of access to become lack of understanding, so I built scrappy versions of those layers myself. My personal projects became laboratories for the backend of the products I was already thinking about at work: APIs, databases, data models, authentication, external services, deployment, and how information moves through a product. I was not trying to recreate a bank’s enterprise architecture in my spare time. I wanted enough understanding to make mistakes safely, recognize tradeoffs, and ask better questions of the engineers, architects, product leaders, security teams, and operators who live in those layers every day. When someone I respect exposes a gap in how I think, I tend to go build something that forces me to understand it.

Connecting the Dots

Today my work reaches well beyond treasury operations, into product, technology, automation, and AI. I lead complex commercial banking implementations and work through the product and technical requirements that come with them. I have built common operating models across our treasury products, automated reporting across hundreds of service records, and created applications and workflows that replaced manual processes. A big part of my job is helping decide how new capabilities should function once they meet a regulated business, and moving internal ideas from “we should fix this” to something people can use. When the same problem shows up more than once, I start looking for the product, workflow, or operating model hiding underneath it.

AI has made that way of working much more powerful, but it has also made judgment more important. I am interested in what AI can do, and even more interested in whether it should be doing it, what information it is using, who owns the outcome, how its work is checked, and whether the people and processes around it are ready. That starts before anyone writes a prompt. I work with business teams to understand the current workflow and source systems, the volume and expected value, the failure points and risks, and what adoption will require. Sometimes AI belongs in the answer. Sometimes it does not.

When it does, I think about the entire product around the model: what context it needs, where that context should come from, how retrieval should work, what belongs in deterministic logic instead, where human review is necessary, how quality is measured, and what happens when the system is wrong. In a regulated environment those questions are not peripheral. They are the product. Access and permissions, PII and data handling, auditability, testing, approval status, and system-of-record boundaries all have to survive contact with the real organization, and so does human accountability.

One principle I rely on constantly is information entropy. If a system depends on authoritative information, I want the authoritative source to remain authoritative. I do not want important knowledge copied into unmanaged documents, prompts, or repositories that slowly drift away from the source of truth. AI should retrieve from current, approved information wherever possible, and ownership should be clear. This is why I do not see governance as the thing that comes after innovation. Good governance is architecture. It is what lets useful ideas move beyond demos and experiments into capabilities people can trust, operate, measure, and scale.

Much of my role now happens in the spaces between disciplines. Commercial and technical product each understand part of the picture, while engineering, operations, security, risk, compliance, and the bankers and customers themselves see other pieces. My job is rarely just to carry information between them. It is to understand enough of each side to challenge assumptions, expose dependencies, and define what matters, then help the group turn a messy business problem into something that can be built and run. It also means passing on what I have learned. Just as one colleague got me started, I helped another get comfortable building with AI and Power Automate, and he has since built some seriously impressive tools that speed up our processes at work.

Steve Jobs once said, “You can’t connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards.” He was talking about a calligraphy class he took simply because it interested him, which later shaped the Macintosh’s typography. I understand the feeling. Computers and paintball, Munich and Aristotle, banking and massage school, treasury and AI did not feel connected while I was living through them. What connects them is an instinct and a lesson. The instinct is the one I started with: I take things apart until I understand them, and now I build things for the same reason. The lesson took longer, and Aristotle gave me the words for it. In the Nicomachean Ethics, each virtue sits between two extremes: courage between cowardice and recklessness. A life lived in that balance is the path to eudaimonia, a word usually translated as happiness but meaning something closer to flourishing. PPP taught me what happens when that balance breaks, and the best teams I have been part of showed me what it looks like when high standards and support hold together.

I know the kind of work I want to keep doing. I want to build and run businesses, products, systems, and teams that work well, where people are challenged and supported, and where technology makes them more capable without replacing their judgment. I want to be the person people count on when something is complicated, stuck, or simply not working as well as it could. I have done that inside banks, in new ventures, and on my own projects. I would like to do more of it for other businesses, whether that means building something of my own or helping run something bigger. Looking backward, the dots already connect more than I expected.
