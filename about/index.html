A big about me

How I got here...

Relentless curiosity

I have been building computers and teaching myself software since I was nine years old. If you’ve gotten to know me, you might have heard me say that. It is an easy way to explain something that has followed me through nearly everything I have done: when I encounter something I do not understand, I tend to take it apart until I do.

In fifth grade, our computer class was assigned a website project built entirely in HTML. I coded mine at home in one night, saved it to a floppy disk, and brought it to school the next day. Once I finished, I started helping my friends build theirs. Around the same time, I rebuilt my Dell into a Thermaltake case and became active in one of the better-known computer-modding communities online, learning from and trading ideas with people who were among the best in the scene. Most of them had no idea they were talking to a kid.

Sports were developing another side of me at the same time. My dad coached, and I grew up in a family that was intensely competitive but equally supportive. Soccer, speed training, and plyometrics were simply part of life. I regularly trained alongside athletes several years older than me and sometimes outperformed high school players in the speed drills. I learned early that being the least experienced person in the room was not necessarily a disadvantage. It was often where I learned the fastest.

I started working early, too. By twelve, I was helping in my parents’ warehouse in San Diego, unloading 54-foot shipping containers and sometimes climbing all the way into the back because I was small enough to fit where the adults could not. Competitive paintball became a major part of my life around the same time. I played competitively and worked as a referee at Mr. Paintball in Escondido. One of my more memorable assignments was managing a group of more than twenty officers from the Escondido Police Department for the day. They had a blast, and so did I. A few years later, I helped build Raza Field, San Diego Dynasty’s own field, including installing safety netting from a boom lift roughly thirty feet in the air.

At the time, none of this felt especially unusual to me. I liked being around people who were better than I was, asking questions, testing myself, and figuring out how things worked. I was also growing up around people who competed hard without treating someone else’s success as a threat. Teaching, encouragement, and high expectations could coexist. That combination has stayed with me.

Starting over

My teenage years were not particularly linear. I lost my best friend during my freshman year of high school and failed the year. The following year, after moving to a charter school, I earned a 4.0. I never completed a traditional junior year because my family’s life was increasingly divided between the United States, Germany, and Sweden, and I moved to Munich.

My first stretch there lasted about six months. I studied German at the Goethe-Institut and attended a German high school before returning to California, where I earned my GED. About a year later, I went back to Germany for what was supposed to be a visit. I was offered a temporary position at a Montessori school for a couple of weeks, but it went well enough that they asked me to stay for the rest of the school year. I did, spending another six months in Munich working with the students and helping teach English before returning to the United States at eighteen.

What those years gave me was a lot of practice starting over. New schools, new countries, new languages, new jobs, and very different groups of people became normal fairly early. I became comfortable walking into situations where I did not know everything yet, paying attention, learning quickly, and finding a way to contribute.

People

Back in California, I sold shoes at Robert Wayne, worked as a valet in Coronado, and eventually found my way into banking as a teller. None of those jobs looked connected at the time, but together they gave me an education in people. I learned how differently people communicate, how quickly a situation can change, and how much trust can depend on seemingly small details. I also discovered that I genuinely enjoyed being responsible for making something work well for someone else.

College gave those instincts another dimension. A rhetoric course introduced me to Aristotle, which eventually led me through Nicomachean Ethics, Marcus Aurelius, Plato, and a much broader interest in how people reason, communicate, and make decisions. I became the only tutor on campus dedicated to business administration and finance and found that teaching forced me to understand ideas at a deeper level. If I could explain something clearly enough for another person to use it, I knew I understood it myself. I ultimately graduated magna cum laude.

My perspective has also been shaped by living and working across cultures. I am a Swedish citizen, spent significant time in Munich, and later worked in Abu Dhabi. My family is based in Sweden and Germany, and international travel has been a regular part of my life, giving me the opportunity to spend time in and learn from communities around the world. Those experiences taught me to listen before assuming I understand the environment I have entered and to expect that intelligent people may approach the same problem very differently.

Systems

My professional career grew from that foundation. I moved from the teller line through personal banking, small-business banking, commercial banking, customer success, treasury operations, complex implementations, and product delivery. Over time, my focus shifted from operating within existing processes to understanding why they worked the way they did, where they were failing, and how the underlying product or operating model could be improved.

Today, I operate at the intersection of treasury, product, technology, automation, and AI transformation. I lead complex commercial banking implementations, but implementation is only one part of the work. I move from customer and banker needs through discovery, requirements, operating-model design, workflow automation, internal applications, executive reporting, governance, adoption, and measurement.

I have designed common operating models across treasury products, built automated reporting across more than 450 treasury-service records, created repeatable applications and workflows to replace manual processes, and established governance and value frameworks across a growing portfolio of AI and automation initiatives. The goal is rarely just to solve the problem in front of me. When I see the same problem more than once, I start looking for the product, workflow, or operating model hiding underneath it.

Much of my role is translation, but not translation in the passive sense. I work across commercial product, technical product, technology, operations, information security, risk, compliance, marketing, and customer-facing teams to turn business needs into things that can actually be delivered. That means defining customer impact and requirements, challenging unclear assumptions, identifying dependencies, establishing readiness and success measures, and helping determine what should be built, in what order, and how it will operate once it exists.

AI has made that work both more interesting and more consequential. I lead discovery with business teams to understand the current workflow, source systems, volume, expected value, risks, and adoption needs before deciding whether AI belongs in the solution at all. From there, I have structured AI product concepts, designed prompt and retrieval frameworks, defined human-review points and quality checks, built internal tools, and helped teams move from experimentation toward capabilities that can operate responsibly inside a regulated business.

Governance is central to how I approach that work. I design against information entropy by keeping authoritative sources authoritative. AI systems should retrieve from current, approved content rather than depend on unmanaged copies that drift away from the source of truth. Ownership should remain clear. Human review should exist where judgment matters. Access, PII, auditability, data handling, system-of-record boundaries, testing, limitations, and approval status should be designed into the product rather than added after the fact.

I do not view governance as something that slows innovation down. Done well, it is what makes innovation safe enough to scale.

Connecting the dots

The common thread through all of this is that I like working between disciplines. Customer and technology. Product and operations. Strategy and execution. Innovation and control. I tend to be most useful when the problem is still a little messy, several people understand different pieces of it, and someone needs to understand enough of each side to connect them and move the work forward.

I have also carried forward something from the coaches, teachers, colleagues, and family members who expected a lot from me early in life. High standards and support are not opposites. The best environments I have been part of challenged people while also giving them the tools, trust, and room to improve. I think good leadership works the same way, and I think good technology should do something similar. It should make people more capable, remove unnecessary friction, and preserve human judgment where that judgment matters.

I know the kind of work I want to keep doing. I want to build and run businesses, products, and teams that work well, and be the person people can count on when something needs to be fixed, simplified, or made better. Whether that eventually means building something of my own or helping run something bigger, I am less concerned with the title than the work itself.

Looking backward, the dots already connect more than I expected.
