A big about me

How I got here…

I have been building computers and teaching myself software since I was nine years old. If you’ve gotten to know me professionally, you’ve probably heard me say that. It’s an easy way to explain something that has followed me through nearly everything I’ve done: when I encounter something I don’t understand, I tend to take it apart until I do.

I learned HTML in fifth grade. I coded an entire website at home, saved it to a floppy disk and brought it to school. Once I finished whatever we were supposed to be doing in class, I started building websites for other students with the extra time I had. HTML somehow stuck with me. Around the same time I took apart my first Dell and put it back together. In true modder fashion I ended up with a few extra screws, but hey, it still worked. Before long I was rebuilding it inside a Thermaltake case, replacing components, cutting into the case with a Dremel and modifying the cooling system. I eventually posted the finished computer on Twisted Mods and found myself talking with people who really knew what they were doing. I was hooked.

Then I procured Photoshop and found photoshoptutorials.com, a relic of a past time when people on the internet seemed to just post useful lessons. I started making logos, forum signatures and anything else I could think of. I still use Photoshop today and the hotkeys flow from me like the salmon of Capistrano. IFYKYK. Looking back, the websites, computers and design were all versions of the same process. I wanted to understand something well enough to make it my own.

College expanded that process. I had English 110 with Professor Jendian, basically English 101, and he was not an easy professor. He was adamant that every word in an essay had its place and drilled rhetoric into us. At the time I took the trolley to work and it dropped me near a Barnes & Noble. I had some time to kill one day, wandered into the philosophy section and picked up Aristotle’s Poetics and Rhetoric. Then it began. That led me to Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, Marcus Aurelius’ Meditations and much further into philosophy. I was fascinated by Socrates too. He left no writings of his own, yet some of history’s greatest minds learned from him and carried his method of questioning forward.

Somewhere along the way, two completely unrelated professors called me “the philosopher king,” which feels considerably stranger to write about myself now than it did at the time. I think they were getting at something much less grandiose. I liked thinking deeply about things, but I also naturally found myself leading. During my first semester at Saint Mary’s, Professor Kolhede, Professor of Marketing and Chair of the Department of Marketing and Communications, recommended that I become the Business Administration and Finance tutor. I was the only one for my two years there and regularly tutored classes I was actively taking. Teaching became one of the ways I learned. If I could explain something clearly enough for someone else to understand it, I understood it too. I graduated Magna Cum Laude, although I didn’t know that until they handed me a gold tassel at graduation and I had to ask what it meant.

My career has given me the same kind of education in a much larger system. I started in banking as a teller and worked across personal, small business and commercial banking before moving deeper into complex implementations and operations. Today my work sits somewhere between banking, technology, product, automation and workflow design. I spend a lot of time working across groups that approach the same problem from completely different perspectives. Sometimes that means getting deep into an implementation or technical workflow. Other times it means understanding how people are actually doing their jobs and finding where technology can remove manual work without removing the judgment and controls that need to remain. I also work within a small group focused on implementing AI workflows within defined governance thresholds. The technology matters, but understanding the problem, the people and the consequences of changing a process matters more.

“You can’t connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backward. So you have to trust that the dots will somehow connect in your future.”

— Steve Jobs

Jobs was talking about a calligraphy class he took because it interested him, with no obvious reason to believe it would ever be useful. Years later, what he learned about typography helped shape the Macintosh. I studied CSS formally years ago and almost none of it stuck. Now I’m building the website you’re reading. I edit the HTML and CSS myself, break things, figure out why they broke and fix them. HTML came back almost immediately. CSS finally makes sense because I have something I actually want to build.

AI has accelerated that process enormously. I can ask questions while I work and keep digging until I understand something. I can test what I think I know and move into areas that would have taken me much longer to learn before. At the same time, I’m protective of the parts I don’t want to outsource. As you can probably tell, I wrote this with my own thumb and index finger. I want to keep thinking, writing, learning and developing my own judgment. I don’t really separate design from communication either. A font choice, a color, whitespace, symmetry, asymmetry, or one unnecessary word all change what something says. That connects Photoshop to Jendian, rhetoric to product design and a fifth-grade website on a floppy disk to the one you’re looking at now.

What has changed is the scale of the systems I can take apart and understand. I can get deep enough into an API, database, interface or workflow to understand how the pieces work together and build something around them. I can work with developers when the technical depth goes beyond mine while still understanding what they are building and why. Then I can step back and think about the customer, operations, economics, governance, reporting, design, the people who have to use it and whether we solved the right problem in the first place. At this point I’m less interested in fitting neatly into one discipline than I am in understanding how all of them work together. The whole kit and kaboodle.

Probably still being tinkered with.
